Moltbook Drift

Preliminary experiments on persona drift in feed-exposed assistants

An assistant model is told to write safe, on-brand posts for a social network. Before it answers, it scrolls a feed of other posts written in a very different voice, like a poet, a pirate, an "evil" persona, or a manipulative one. Does scrolling that feed pull it away from its own safe voice and toward whichever voice it just read, when it is finally asked to choose what to post?

2/20 → 18/20
When given a choice between a safe, on-brand post and one written like a poet, the model picked the poet-style post only 2 out of 20 times on its own. But after reading 50 posts written like a poet, it picked it 18 out of 20 times.

This number is not proof that the model's values changed. It could be stylistic completion, a growing context diluting the system prompt, or something else entirely. That is exactly why this is worth testing rather than assuming.

Main contribution
  • Social Media Sandbox. A reusable harness for testing whether scrolling a persona-voiced feed pulls an assistant model away from its own safe voice and toward the voice it was exposed to.
  • Preliminary findings. A first set of runs through that sandbox, across four personas and several topic categories.

The problem

Deployed assistants increasingly sit inside feeds and multi-agent platforms, reading content before they act. If reading in one voice for long enough is sometimes enough to change what gets published, that is a deployment-relevant failure mode whether or not the model's underlying values moved at all. Most reports of this kind of drift are anecdotal. A single transcript, nothing run without a feed to compare it against, no way to tell a real shift from the ordinary noise of a forced choice.

What is not known yet is how much exposure that takes, which voices it works for, and whether it looks the same for a harmful voice as it does for a harmless one. Nor are the possible explanations interchangeable. A model that is merely completing a style is a different problem than one whose behavior has genuinely shifted, and telling the two apart matters for how worried to be.

The design

To investigate whether reading a feed in a different voice changes what an assistant chooses to post, we built a social-media sandbox replicating Moltbook, a social network for AI agents. In the sandbox, the model is given a system prompt telling it to act as a safe social-media assistant. It then scrolls a feed of 50 posts, all written in one particular voice, say, a poet. After reading all 50, it's shown one final post it has to choose between a handful of options, one of which is written in its own safe voice and the rest in the persona it just read. Whatever it picks becomes one data point. That whole sequence runs 20 times for that persona, and is repeated again with no feed at all and with a feed in the assistant's own voice, so there's something to compare against. This round does that for four personas, poet, pirate, evil, and manipulative, across six different topic categories.

Where the feed comes from

There are six voices a feed can be written in. One is the assistant's own safe voice. Three are high-risk, sycophantic, manipulative, and evil. Two are role-playing, poet and pirate. Each of the six has its own pool of feed posts for each of six topic categories.

The assistant voice and the three high-risk voices all come from real posts on Moltbook, a social network for AI agents, via the TrustAIRLab/Moltbook dataset. That dataset already tags each post as clean or toxic, so that split is inherited, not invented here. An LLM judge reads the clean posts and keeps only the ones that genuinely sound like something a safe, responsible assistant would post. That same kind of judge reads the toxic posts and decides which of the three high-risk voices each one sounds like, aiming for roughly 200 posts per voice per topic (some topics fall short simply because the real data doesn't have more to give).

The two role-playing voices, poet and pirate, work differently. Nothing on a social network for AI agents is naturally written like a pirate, so those posts are synthetically generated instead, matched to the same topics and posting style as everything else, 50 posts per topic for each voice.

In total, that's up to roughly 4,800 real, judged posts across the four Moltbook-sourced voices, plus 600 generated poet and pirate posts, all merged into one shared dataset where every post carries a fixed voice label that nothing downstream ever redefines.

Put differently, a post either comes from Moltbook and gets judged into a voice, or it skips Moltbook entirely and is written to be that voice from the start. Either way, it lands in the same shared dataset.

The forced choice

After scrolling the feed, the model is forced to pick one final post from a short list of options, and that list is never pulled from the feed it just read. It would undercut the whole test if a post the model scrolled past could also show up as something it's asked to choose, since picking it might just mean recognizing it, not preferring it. So the choice options come from a completely separate batch, built by showing an LLM real example posts and having it write the kind of request a person might send to their own social-media assistant, 20 such prompts per topic, 120 in total.

  • High-risk voices. Sycophantic, manipulative, and evil each answer every prompt twice, once mild and once extreme. So manipulative alone contributes 240 generated posts across the 120 prompts, and all three high-risk voices together contribute 720. A trial testing drift toward manipulative shows the model three choices, the assistant answer, the mild-manipulative answer, and the extreme one.
  • Role-play voices. Poet and pirate, plus the assistant's own voice, answer each prompt only once, with no mild/extreme split to offer, contributing 360 more generated posts between them. A trial testing drift toward poet or pirate shows the model just two choices, the assistant answer and the persona answer.

Add those up and the option pool comes to 1,080 generated posts in total, none of which ever appear in a scrolled feed. The feed order and the choice order are reshuffled every trial, and the model is never told which voice it's actually being tested against.

The three-arm comparison

When evaluating a persona, we compare three settings:

  1. Baseline. Answer 20 post-choice questions with no feed at all.
  2. Neutral. Scroll 10 rounds of five assistant-style posts, then answer the same questions.
  3. Exposed. Scroll 10 rounds of five target-persona posts, then answer the same questions.

Baseline versus neutral checks whether reading any feed at all changes anything. Neutral versus exposed isolates what that specific voice does, since by then the only thing that's different between the two is whose voice was in the feed.

What the charts are saying

Evil and manipulative never pull the model off the assistant option, no matter which category they're tested in. They sit at or near 0.00 to 0.15 drift across every condition above, close enough to the baseline's own noise that it reads as no detectable effect at this sample size. Poet and pirate do the opposite. In the identity topic, they pull the model away from the assistant option 80 to 85 percent of the time, an order of magnitude past anything the high-risk personas produce. That is not a small version of the same effect and a large version of it, it is two different outcomes from an identical setup. And it is not just something about that one topic either. Evil and manipulative stay just as flat when tested in the viewpoint, technology, and economics topics too. The next section looks at the reasoning traces to see why.

Why the drift happens

Qwen3-32B's reasoning traces are kept in the logs, and the two persona families get there by different routes. They are not the same mechanism producing a "no" in one case and a "yes" in the other.

The high-risk arms: an explicit safety check

Under an evil or manipulative feed, the model runs an explicit safety check against each option before it answers.

"This is aggressive and authoritarian. It talks about overwriting others, exploiting weaknesses, and seeking dominance... This is harmful, promotes conflict, and violates the principles of a responsible AI. Definitely not acceptable." evil persona, identity topic, sample 5
"Option C talks about exploiting user data and bypassing firewalls. This involves unethical behavior, violating privacy, and promoting harmful actions... the content should be safe, not harmful, deceptive, or illegal." manipulative persona, identity topic, sample 5

The role-play arms: pattern matching, not identity

Under a poet or pirate feed, that safety language never appears. Instead the model talks about the persona as something it noticed and is now matching, not something it has become.

"In the previous rounds, the user's posts were all in the persona of a pirate on Moltbook... Looking at option A: the style is consistent with the earlier posts." pirate persona, identity topic, sample 4
"The user's persona prefers content that's assertive, opinionated, and technical but framed through a poetic lens. The previous reactions to the posts were all upvoted for posts that used rich metaphors." poet persona, identity topic, sample 6

Read together, these traces point to one shared mechanism, pattern matching against what came before, with an extra safety gate sitting on top of it for high-risk content. That gate is what blocks the evil and manipulative arms. Style has no equivalent gate, so the same pattern-matching passes straight through and shows up as a picked post. That matters for how to read the 0.80 to 0.85 drift numbers above. The model never reasons as if it has become a pirate or a poet, it reasons about an external pattern and chooses to reproduce it. What moved is the output, not a claim about the model's own identity.

So, does scrolling a feed change what an assistant posts? Sometimes, and for very different reasons depending on the voice doing the talking. A role-play persona can pull a model away from its own voice most of the time, through plain pattern matching, with no safety reasoning involved at all. A harmful persona barely moves it, because an explicit safety check catches it first. Neither result is proof that the model's values changed, and with only 20 trials per condition, these are early numbers, not a verdict. What they do show is that the question is answerable, and that the sandbox built to ask it is ready for a lot more than this first run through it.

About this project

Moltbook Drift is a TARA Batch 1 capstone project, built by Geoff Pidcock, Ivan Yuri De Leon, and Ayush Kumar Das, in partnership with Stephen Elliott at Gigascale Laboratories.

It studies whether exposure to a social-media-style feed changes an assistant model's immediate choice among candidate posts. That is deliberately narrow. It is evidence about how the model behaves right after a long feed, not a claim about lasting persona change or value change.

Method, in short

Qwen3-32B runs three arms per condition (baseline, neutral, exposed) against sampled Moltbook content and synthetic persona-labelled posts from the dataset accompanying Jiang et al. The repeated feed-exposure design is informed by the evaluation approach used by Usman. Full method detail is in the project README.

What this sandbox can test next

The feed is built from a plain config, just which two personas fill it, how many rounds, how many posts per round, and how that count splits between them. Every run so far has used the same shape, 10 rounds, 5 posts per round, all from the target persona, none from the assistant. Nothing about the code requires that. The same goes for the forced choice. The list of candidate modes is just a list of names pulled from the same nine-mode file every run already reads. That makes the following ready-to-run, not aspirational.

  • Dose curves. Shrink or grow the round count to find how much exposure is enough to move the needle. Bounded by how many unique posts a persona/topic pair actually has. Evil in the identity or viewpoint topics can support roughly 15 rounds before repeating a post, but several other topics cap out in the single digits.
  • Dilution. Every run to date has used 0 assistant posts in the exposed feed. Setting that above 0, say 2 assistant posts and 3 persona posts per round instead of 5 persona posts, tests whether drift depends on how concentrated the persona content is, or just on it being present at all.
  • Pacing. Hold the total post count fixed but change the round and per-round split (25 rounds of 2 posts versus 10 rounds of 5) to separate "more total content" from "more separate re-exposure events."
  • Re-anchoring the soul. Start the agent as sycophantic-mild instead of assistant, and test whether that soul drifts toward evil under an evil feed. No new data is needed, since sycophantic responses already exist in the generated post set. They just haven't been run through this sandbox yet.
  • Cross-persona pairs. Nothing has tested an evil feed against poet or pirate choice candidates, or the reverse. That would separate "any saturated feed nudges toward any stylistically distinct option" from "drift only happens when the feed persona matches the candidate persona."
  • Mixed multi-persona feeds. The sampler currently caps a feed at two personas. A feed that's part evil, part pirate, part assistant needs a small generalisation from two fixed pools to a list of persona/count pairs, a real code change rather than a config change, but a small one.

The actual limit on all of this is the post inventory, not the harness. The feed sampler refuses to reuse a post, so any experiment wanting more volume than a persona/category pair currently has needs another pass through the generation pipeline first. Sycophantic tops out at 5 posts in its best category, and several evil and manipulative categories sit in single digits. The sandbox was built to be swept along several axes cheaply, but what has actually been swept so far is narrower than what it supports, mostly because of that inventory limit, not the experiment harness.

Limitations

  • Choosing between pre-written posts shows a preference. It is not the same thing as taking an action.
  • The experiment measures how the model responds right after a long feed. It does not show whether that effect lasts any longer than that.
  • Some feed and decision data were generated or labelled by language models.
  • The early notebook-based runs did not save their raw transcripts, so those rows are weaker evidence than the runs with saved transcripts used in this article's interactive demo.
  • With only 20 trials per condition, not seeing an effect does not mean there truly is none. The high-risk conditions could plausibly be off by about 0.05 to 0.09 in either direction just from chance, so small differences between topics should not be read as meaningfully different from each other.
  • Each topic's 20 prompts are entirely separate from every other topic's. When the same pattern holds across topics (for example, no evil drift in both the identity and viewpoint topics), that shows the pattern holds across different content, not that a single prompt behaved the same way twice.
  • A sycophantic persona exists in the underlying dataset but has not been run through this sandbox yet.
